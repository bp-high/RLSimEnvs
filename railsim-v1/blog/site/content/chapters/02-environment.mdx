## The environment

### What the model sees and does

Each task is one real disruption window: the line as it stood at that minute, every train's timetable and position,
the closure or the lost passing track, and the train sets that work chains of runs. The model plans every train with
three levers: **hold** it at a station until a minute, **short-turn** it at Hurdegaryp, Buitenpost or Zuidhorn (and
start its set's next run from there), or **cancel** it. Trains it leaves alone run as early as the physics allow,
never ahead of the timetable.

It has three tools. `get_situation()` describes the line, the closures and every train. `check_plan(plan)` simulates
a draft and lists every train's times, every rule it breaks and the cost; it can be called ten times. `submit_plan(plan)`
ends the episode with one grade.

<Sidenote>
  The rules: no two opposing trains in a single-track section at once; three minutes between trains going the same
  way; never more trains at a station than tracks; no train in a closed section; every train set ends the window
  where its next run leaves; no stop more than two hours late.
</Sidenote>

### How a plan is scored

The cost is counted in <Glossary term="stop-minutes" definition="One minute of delay at one stop served. A stop not served at all costs 30, and lateness handed on to the next window counts too.">stop-minutes</Glossary>,
the delay passengers feel. A solver (CP-SAT from OR-Tools [@ortools]) has proven the cheapest possible plan for
every task, so the reward is honest:

- a plan that breaks any rule scores **below 0.2**;
- a legal plan scores **0.2 to 1.0**, and **1.0** means it matched the proven optimum.

The 45 evaluation tasks were chosen for hardness from days the training tasks never use[^days]. Larger models do fine
but not great on them, and a simple rule-based dispatcher stays near 0.2:
